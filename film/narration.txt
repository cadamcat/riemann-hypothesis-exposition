Primes and their error

The prime numbers thin out as they grow. Up to x there are about x over log x of them, and the logarithmic integral, Li of x, follows their count far more closely. In 1859 Riemann tied the error to the zeros of one function, the zeta function. His hypothesis says where those zeros lie. If it holds, the error stays below a constant times the square root of x, times log x, for large x. On 20 November 2026, L. Alpöge published a short note claiming a proof of the Riemann hypothesis. The note says the proof was found working with Claude. With the note came a proof in Lean. On 21 November 2026, the Lean FRO and the Mathlib Initiative certified it as correct, statement and proof, and independent checkers accept it.

Zeta and its zeros

Where the real part of s is greater than one, zeta of s is the sum of one over n to the power s. It extends to the whole complex plane, with a single pole, at s equals one. It vanishes at minus two, minus four, minus six, and so on: the trivial zeros. Every other zero lies in the critical strip, where the real part is between zero and one. Here is the size of zeta over the strip, computed. The first zeros sit at heights 14.13, 21.02 and 25.01, each on the line where the real part is one half. The Riemann hypothesis says that every non-trivial zero lies on this line. Mathlib, the mathematical library written by Lean's community, states it this way: every s where zeta vanishes, other than minus two, minus four and so on, and other than one, has real part one half.

What the source states

The Lean source is large: 175,187 files in ten libraries. Its root file, Main.lean, exports five theorems. The first has as its type exactly Mathlib's proposition, the Riemann hypothesis. The second states the same proposition in the source's own words. The other three concern Dirichlet L-functions. A Dirichlet character modulo N, chi, is multiplicative, repeats with period N, and is zero on numbers that share a factor with N. Its L-function is the sum of chi of n over n to the s, continued like zeta. Zeta is the case N equals one. All five theorems rest on one proposition: no Dirichlet L-function vanishes where the real part of s is greater than one half, except at s equals one for a principal character. The step from it to the five theorems is short and classical. For zeta, the functional equation mirrors any non-trivial zero left of the line to a zero right of it.

The analytic half

Below that proposition, the proof has two halves. The analytic half works with primes through lambda of n, the von Mangoldt function: log p when n is a power of a prime p, and zero otherwise. The source sums lambda of n times x over n minus one, for n up to x, then subtracts x log x and adds one plus gamma, times x; gamma is Euler's constant. Call this G of x. Classically, G has an explicit formula: each non-trivial zero rho of zeta contributes a wave, x to the rho over rho times rho minus one. Here is the exact G, computed, against the waves of the first zeros. With more zeros, they follow G ever more closely. A zero with real part beta makes a wave of size x to the beta. If every zero is on the line, G stays of size root x. A zero to the right of the line would force larger swings. The source turns this around, for suitable finite families of Dirichlet characters, with their weighted prime sum. Take theta between zero and one, and delta positive. If the sum stays below a constant times y to the theta on windows from Y to Y to the one plus delta, for arbitrarily large Y, then no L-function of the family vanishes right of theta. The bound is one-sided and needed only on sparse windows. Classically, one-sided bounds exclude zeros through positivity, as in Landau's theorem; which positivity the source uses, the article leaves open. The criterion is fed by a square-root bound stated in the source: on the windows of suitably compatible auxiliary families, the sum eventually stays below x to the one half plus a small multiple of epsilon. The difficulty moves into producing those families.

The two halves

Here is the top of the proof, as the source writes it. The Riemann hypothesis rests on the every-character theorem. On the left is the analytic half; on the right, the automorphic half, which supplies the families. Each box is one Lean declaration. Beneath them lie the ten libraries; the largest, Development, has about 142,000 files and 12 million lines. The automorphic half rests on four main inputs, named here but not proved. First, the window bound: lengths of Tate–Shafarevich groups, Sha, from Galois cohomology, bounded over fields cut out of cyclotomic fields, the fields of roots of unity. Second, Galois representations for polarized, regular algebraic cuspidal representations over CM fields. Third, a form of the local Langlands correspondence for GL n. Fourth, theta lifts between unitary groups, in a stable range. Each has the shape of published theorems. Except for local Langlands, how the stated form relates to them is a question the article leaves to experts. Both halves meet in one theorem: the families arrive, the square-root bound holds on windows, and the criterion clears every primitive character's L-function right of one half. Zeta is simply the case of the trivial character.

The author's idea

As the note puts it, Stepanov's proof of the Riemann hypothesis for curves over finite fields uses a divisor with multiplicity. Over the integers, multiplicity is hard to find; it is something like differentiating whole numbers. Its approach is to chain theta lifts, each one mimicking multiplicity. For a von Mangoldt sum of length X, the note says, Claude suggested a chain of about root X lifts. In the source, the prime sum is exactly the note's, and a single theta lift is a stated theorem. The article has not located the chain or its length; no name in the tree contains Stepanov.

Where the check stands

On 21 November 2026, the Lean FRO and the Mathlib Initiative certified that both are correct: the statement and the proof. Experts vetted Mathlib's statement. Dozens of builders reproduced the same exported proof. Two tools matched the proved theorem to that statement. Several checkers, Lean's kernel among them, accept the proof. The certification covers both halves: the statement, Mathlib's definition, is correct, and the proof of exactly that statement is valid. It does not explain the argument. That is for mathematicians to read and explain. On 20 November, Ravi Vakil posted on his blog an independent, human-written manuscript of about 940 pages by Alessandro Masullo, claiming a proof of the generalized Riemann hypothesis; Brian Conrad had reviewed it for months. The Riemann hypothesis now has a Lean proof, certified as correct. What remains is for mathematicians: to read the argument, and to explain its ideas.
